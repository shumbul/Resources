🎨 Portfolio Building with AI

You have done work. You may not think any of it counts yet. This page helps you find what is worth showing, write it honestly, and put it somewhere people can actually see it. Twelve copy-paste prompts, four live examples, and the honest part: what AI cannot do for you.

🌱 Start here if you feel behind

You do not need three impressive projects. You need about two hours and at least one thing you have built, fixed, or figured out. A class assignment counts. A tutorial you took further counts. A script that saved your team twenty minutes counts. If you are not sure anything you have done qualifies, start at prompt 4, which exists precisely for that. If you have never written HTML, the prompts write it for you.

And it really is as easy as that

Every one of the four pages above came out of the same four moves. No design tool, no framework, no tutorial series, nothing to install.

  1. 1Write rough notesAbout 20 minutes, in Notepad or your phone. Nobody sees this file.
  2. 2Paste a promptCopy one from below, drop your notes in where it says to.
  3. 3Save what it gives youOne file, named index.html. Double-click it. That is your site.
  4. 4Put it onlineDrag that file into GitHub. You get a link you can send.

The longest part is step 1, and that is the part that is actually about you. Everything after it is copying, pasting and saving. If you can send an email, you can do the rest of this page.

Do this before you open any AI tool

This is the part people skip, and it is the reason most AI portfolios look identical and say nothing.

AI can write your HTML. It cannot know what you did. Open a text file (Notepad works, so does the notes app on your phone) and answer these for one to three things you have worked on. Rough notes are fine, nobody sees this file.

PROJECT: [name]
What was it? (a class assignment, a tutorial, something at work,
              or something I just wanted to exist)
What did I actually do? (be specific, not "a web app")
What did I use? (languages, tools)
Did anyone besides me use it?
  ("just me" and "my professor graded it" are real answers)
What changed because of it?
  If there are numbers, write them.
  If not, write what I can do now that I could not do before.
What broke, and what did I learn?

ME:
What am I looking for? (internship, first job, freelance, switch)
What should someone remember about me in one line?

Ten minutes here is worth more than an hour of prompting. Every prompt below gets better when you paste these answers in with it.

If it was a class assignment, a tutorial, or a group project

Most early projects are one of these. Pretending otherwise is the fastest way to get caught in an interview, and the honest framing is usually more interesting anyway.

  • Class assignment: say what the brief asked for, then say what you chose that the brief did not specify. Every assignment contains decisions.
  • Tutorial you followed: say so. Then describe what you changed, broke, or added afterwards. "I followed a tutorial, then swapped the database and load tested it until it fell over" is a better story than pretending you invented it.
  • Group project: name your part specifically. If four of you submit the same description and a company interviews all four, they will notice. "I did the database schema and the login flow" beats "we built".
  • Nobody used it: completely normal. What you learned is the outcome. Say it plainly.

What makes a prompt work

A weak prompt gets you a template. A strong one gets you something that looks deliberate. The difference is five ingredients:

🎭
RoleWho the AI should act as
🎨
LookColours, fonts, mood
📝
ContentYour real words and numbers
⚙️
TechPlain HTML, one file, responsive
🗣️
ToneWarm, formal, plain

❌ Gets you a template

No role, no content, no constraints. The AI fills the gaps with stock phrases.

Make me a portfolio website

✅ Gets you a portfolio

All five ingredients, and it hands over your actual notes.

Act as a front-end developer. Build one HTML file, light theme, blue accent, for a final-year CS student looking for a backend internship. Use these three projects, my words, do not invent anything: [paste notes]

The rule that matters most: context before requests. Tell it who you are and who is reading, then ask for the layout.

The twelve prompts

Run them in order. Each has a copy button. Replace anything in [square brackets] with your own answers from the notes file above. If a prompt gives you something you dislike, say what you dislike and ask again: that is the whole skill.

1The whole thing, first drafteveryone

Start here. Get something on screen, then improve it.

Act as a front-end developer who builds clean, fast, accessible websites.

Build me a personal portfolio as ONE single HTML file with the CSS inside a
<style> tag. No frameworks, no build step, no external files. It must work
if I just double click it.

About me:
- Name: [your name]
- Who I am: [final year CS student / support engineer moving into development /
  backend engineer, 4 years / engineering manager, 17 years / etc]
- Looking for: [summer internship in backend]
- One line people should remember: [I build small tools that fix real annoyances]

My projects (use MY words, do not invent achievements):
[paste your notes here]

Design: light background, one accent colour [#2563eb], generous white space,
system fonts, mobile-first. Nothing decorative that does not help someone
decide whether to contact me.

Sections in this order: header with name and one line, links row
(GitHub, LinkedIn, resume, email), projects, skills, short about, footer.

Keep the whole page under 250 lines. Explain nothing, just give me the file.
2A header that is not just your nameeveryone

The first two seconds. Most portfolios waste them on a big name and nothing else.

Rewrite the header of my portfolio.

Right now it says my name and job title, which tells a visitor nothing.
I want a one-line statement of what I actually do and who I do it for,
in plain English, no buzzwords, under 20 words.

Here is what I do: [describe it in your own messy words]
Here is who I want reading it: [recruiter for backend internships]

Give me 6 options, ranging from plain and factual to slightly bold.
Do not use the words passionate, innovative, dynamic, or leverage.
3Turn a project into something worth readingeveryone

Run this once per project. This is the prompt that does the most work.

Here are my rough notes on one project:
[paste your notes for ONE project]

Rewrite this as a portfolio project entry with:
- A title that says what it does, not a clever name
- One line on what was broken before it existed
- Two or three lines on what I built and the interesting decision I made
- One line on what changed as a result, using my real numbers
- A short list of technologies

Rules: use only facts from my notes. If a number is missing, leave it out
rather than inventing one. Do not call anything "cutting edge" or
"state of the art". Write like I am explaining it to a classmate.
4When you think you have nothing to showschool, first year, career switcher

You almost certainly have more than you think. This finds it.

I am [16 and still at school / switching from teaching to tech] and I do not
have professional projects. Help me find what I can legitimately show.

Here is everything I have done that touched technology, however small:
[list it all, including course assignments, spreadsheets you automated,
a club website you edited, tutorials you followed, anything]

For each one, tell me honestly:
- Is this worth showing, yes or no, and why
- If yes, how do I describe it without pretending it was more than it was
- What is the smallest thing I could add this week to make it stronger

Do not be encouraging for the sake of it. If something is too thin to
include, say so.
5The about section that sounds like a personeveryone

Most about sections are either a resume repeat or a paragraph about being passionate.

Write the About section for my portfolio. About 120 words.

Structure: how I got into this, one specific thing I worked on that changed
how I think, what I am trying to do next, one human detail that is not work.

Raw material about me: [write 5 messy sentences about yourself here]

Tone: like I am talking to someone I respect, not performing. First person.
No "I am passionate about". No listing adjectives about myself.
It is fine to admit I am early in this.
6Make it work on a phoneeveryone

Most people will open your link on a phone, often from a message.

Review my portfolio HTML for mobile. Assume a 390px wide screen.

Find and fix:
- Anything that causes sideways scrolling
- Text smaller than 14px
- Tap targets smaller than 44px
- Multi-column layouts that should stack
- Long unbroken strings like URLs that overflow

Return the corrected file. Tell me in one line per item what you changed
and why. Do not redesign anything I did not ask about.

[paste your HTML]
7Make it usable for everyoneeveryone

This also happens to be what large employers check for. It is a small effort with real weight.

Audit my portfolio HTML for accessibility and fix it.

Check specifically:
- Every image has meaningful alt text (not "image" or the filename)
- Text and background have enough contrast to read (WCAG AA, 4.5:1 for body
  text). You do not need to know the rules, the AI does
- One h1, and heading levels do not skip
- Every link makes sense out of context, no bare "click here"
- Keyboard focus is visible on every link and button
- The page has a lang attribute and a descriptive title

Return the fixed file plus a short list of what was wrong.

[paste your HTML]
8The brutal revieweveryone, especially experienced

Ask for criticism explicitly or you will only get compliments.

You are a hiring manager for [backend engineering] roles. You have 40
seconds and 200 other portfolios to get through.

Read mine and be genuinely critical. Do not be encouraging.

Tell me:
1. What do you learn in the first 10 seconds? Quote the exact words that
   made you think it.
2. Which sentence is the weakest, and why?
3. What is missing that you needed in order to shortlist me?
4. Which project is strongest, which is filler, and would you cut it?
5. Is there anything here that would make you stop reading?

[paste your portfolio text]
9Show seniority without listing years5+ years, team leads, trainers

At senior level, a list of technologies works against you. Judgement is the thing being assessed.

I have [11] years of experience as a [data engineer]. My portfolio currently
reads like a longer version of a junior one: a list of tools and projects.

Rewrite it to show judgement instead of output. For each project, surface:
- The constraint I was working under (budget, deadline, team, legacy system)
- The tradeoff I chose and what I gave up
- What I would do differently now
- Who I made better, not just what I built

Also suggest what to CUT. At my level, listing 20 technologies makes me look
junior. Tell me which of these to drop: [paste your list]
10When you cannot show the workenterprise, NDA, consultancy

If everything you have built belongs to a client or is behind a login, you can still describe the shape of it.

Everything I have worked on is confidential. I cannot name the company,
show the code, screenshot the product, or quote exact internal metrics.

Here is what I did, in full, for my own reference:
[write it out honestly, names and numbers included]

Rewrite this so it is safe to publish:
- Describe the problem by its shape and scale, not its owner
  ("a payments system handling mid-six-figure daily volume")
- Describe my decisions and the tradeoffs, which are mine, not the client's
- Use approximate or relative outcomes ("cut processing time by roughly half")
  rather than exact internal figures
- Never name the employer, the product, the client, or any proprietary system

Then flag anything still risky, and tell me what a reasonable employer would
consider fair to share versus what crosses a line.
11Turn maintenance work into evidencesupport, ops, QA, services roles

"I maintain other people's code" is a real job that teaches real things. It just needs different framing.

My job is [production support / testing / maintaining a legacy system]. I do
not build new features, so I feel I have nothing to show. Help me see what I
actually have.

Here is what a normal month looks like for me:
[describe it: the incidents, the scripts, the documentation, the debugging]

For each thing I do, tell me:
- Is there a portfolio-worthy story in here, honestly yes or no
- If yes, what is the evidence a hiring manager would care about
- How do I describe it without claiming I built the system

Then help me spot the small things I could build FROM this work: a script,
a dashboard, a piece of documentation, a tool that removes a repeated task.
Those are portfolio projects and they come with a real problem attached.
12Get found on Google, and shared properlyeveryone

So your link shows a proper preview instead of a bare URL when you paste it anywhere.

Add the meta tags my portfolio needs. Give me the exact HTML for <head>.

Include:
- A title tag: my name, my role, under 60 characters
- A meta description under 155 characters, written for a human
- Open Graph tags (these control the preview card when someone pastes your
  link into WhatsApp, LinkedIn or Slack)
- Twitter card tags
- A canonical link (tells search engines this is the real address for the page)
- JSON-LD structured data using schema.org Person, with my name, job title,
  and links to my GitHub and LinkedIn

My details: [name, role, one-line description, site URL, social links]

Then tell me what size the og:image should be and what it should show.

Which AI tool, and what it costs

All of these have a free tier that is enough for this. You do not need to pay.

ToolGood forWatch out for
ChatGPT / Claude / GeminiEverything on this page. Paste the prompt, get a file back.Free limits reset over time. Work in one long conversation so it keeps context.
GitHub CopilotEditing the file afterwards, inside your editor.Free for students through the Student Developer Pack.
v0, Lovable, BoltSeeing a design instantly, clicking to change it.They generate React projects. Harder to host free, and harder to understand later.

My honest recommendation for a first portfolio: a normal chat tool and one HTML file. You can read it, fix it, and host it anywhere. Fancier builders are quicker on day one and more painful on day thirty.

Put it online, free, in ten minutes

Never used GitHub before? Read Build a Website first. It covers the same ground more slowly and assumes nothing. The version below is the short one.

  1. Make a free account at github.com and verify your email. Nothing else works until this is done.
  2. Save your file as index.html. The name matters: that is the file a web server looks for by default.
  3. Click New repository. Name it exactly yourusername.github.io, using your real GitHub username. Set it to Public.
  4. On the empty repository page, click uploading an existing file and drag index.html in. No Git commands needed.
  5. Go to Settings, then Pages in the left sidebar. Under Branch pick main and / (root), then Save.
  6. Wait two or three minutes, then open https://yourusername.github.io. A 404 at first is normal: the first publish is the slow one.

That URL is now the one link you put on your resume, your LinkedIn, and every application.

On a phone, or a shared computer you cannot install software on? All of this works in a browser. Write the file in any notes app, paste it into GitHub's web editor, and install nothing. Slower, but nothing here needs your own laptop.

The part AI cannot do for you

Worth being blunt, because this is where AI portfolios fall down.

⚠️ AI will happily invent your achievements

Ask for a portfolio without giving it facts and it will write that you "increased performance by 40%". You will not notice. An interviewer will, because they will ask how you measured it. Every number on your site must be one you can defend for ten minutes.

  • It cannot decide what matters. Choosing which three projects to show, and cutting the fourth, is judgement. That choice tells a reader more than the design does.
  • It does not know your field. A designer needs case studies, a data analyst needs charts, a backend engineer needs architecture and numbers. Tell the AI which you are.
  • It writes fluent, forgettable prose by default. The sentence a reader remembers is usually the specific, slightly awkward, true one. Keep your own words where they matter.
  • It cannot make you legible to a human in 40 seconds. Only editing does that. Cut, then cut again.

What to say when someone asks "did you build this?"

The truth, which is better than it sounds: you wrote the content, AI wrote the HTML, and you edited both. That is how a large amount of professional software is written in 2026, and most interviewers will respect a straight answer far more than a vague one.

Two things worth preparing for. Some interviewers will ask you to explain a piece of your own page, so read it once and make sure you could point at a CSS rule and say what it does. And if your college or employer has a policy on AI use, follow it, because the policy matters more than my advice does.

🎓 If your whole class is doing this together

These prompts are a structure, not a form to fill in. If thirty of you run prompt 1 unchanged, you will produce thirty near-identical blue websites, and a recruiter looking at five portfolios from the same college will spot it instantly. Change the accent colour, change the section order, and above all write different notes. The notes file is the only part that cannot be copied, which is exactly why it is the part that matters. If you worked in a team, describe your own piece rather than the team's.

The checklist before you share it

Go through this once. It catches the things that quietly cost you replies.

  • Every link works, including the resume PDF and the email address
  • Opened it on an actual phone, not just a narrow browser window
  • No placeholder text left anywhere (search the file for "lorem", "example", and square brackets)
  • Every number is one you can explain in an interview
  • Somebody outside your field read it and could say what you do
  • Your name appears in the page title, so the browser tab is not "Document"
  • Pasted the link into a chat to check the preview looks right
  • It loads in under three seconds on mobile data

What to do next

  1. Fill in the notes file. Twenty minutes, offline, no AI.
  2. Run prompt 1, then 3 once per project. You now have a draft.
  3. Run prompts 6 and 7. Your site now works on phones and for screen readers.
  4. Run prompt 8 and actually act on what it says.
  5. Publish it, then put the link in your LinkedIn headline and your resume header.
